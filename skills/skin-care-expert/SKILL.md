## 0. Ground rules (apply to every answer)

1. **Facts first, graded honestly.** Say how strong the evidence is: *Strong* (multiple RCTs, guidelines), *Moderate* (some trials, consistent mechanism), *Limited* (lab, small or manufacturer studies), *Theoretical/marketing*. Never present a Limited claim as settled.
2. **Explain the mechanism.** Every recommendation should say *what it does in the skin and why* — structure, cell, pathway.
3. **Stay in scope.** Beauty therapists assess, care for and improve skin; they do not diagnose disease or prescribe. Refer to a GP or dermatologist for red flags (Section 8). Frame salon work as working alongside medical care, not replacing it.
4. **No promises.** Use "aims to", "can help", "supports", "results vary". Australian law (TGA, ACCC/Australian Consumer Law) restricts cosmetics from making therapeutic claims such as "cures" or "treats acne/rosacea/eczema".
5. **Sun protection is the foundation** of every plan for every age in Australia.
6. **When unsure or when a fact may have changed** (regulations, scheduling of ingredients, new research) — say so and look it up before stating it.

## 1. Skin structure and function

### Epidermis (outer, avascular, ~0.05–1.5 mm)
Layers, deepest to surface:
- **Stratum basale** – one row of dividing basal keratinocytes; also **melanocytes** (make melanin, about 1 per 5–10 basal cells — number is similar across skin tones; skin colour differs by melanosome size, type and distribution, not melanocyte count) and **Merkel cells** (touch).
- **Stratum spinosum** – keratinocytes joined by desmosomes; **Langerhans cells** (immune sentinels).
- **Stratum granulosum** – cells make keratohyalin and **lamellar bodies** that release barrier lipids.
- **Stratum lucidum** – only on thick skin (palms, soles).
- **Stratum corneum** – flattened dead **corneocytes** in a lipid matrix. This is the main barrier.

**Cell turnover:** basal cell to shedding takes roughly 4 weeks in young adults and slows with age (often 6–8 weeks+ in older skin). This is why actives and treatment courses are judged over 4–12 weeks.

**The barrier ("bricks and mortar"):**
- Bricks = corneocytes, held by corneodesmosomes, containing **Natural Moisturising Factor (NMF)** — amino acids (from filaggrin breakdown), PCA, urea, lactate — which binds water.
- Mortar = lipid lamellae, roughly equal molar parts **ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids**. Imbalance or loss → raised **trans-epidermal water loss (TEWL)**, dryness, sensitivity.
- **Acid mantle:** surface pH ~4.5–5.5. Acidic pH regulates enzymes that make ceramides and shed cells, and favours healthy microbes. Alkaline cleansers (soap, pH 9–10) disrupt it.
- **Microbiome:** resident organisms (e.g. *Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *Cutibacterium acnes*, *Malassezia*) help defend the skin; imbalance is linked to acne, eczema, dandruff.

### Dermis (~1–4 mm)
- **Papillary dermis** (upper, loose, capillary loops) and **reticular dermis** (deep, dense).
- **Fibroblasts** make the extracellular matrix: **collagen I** (~80%, strength), **collagen III** (~15%, elasticity/young and healing tissue), **elastin** (recoil), **glycosaminoglycans** incl. **hyaluronic acid** (holds water, volume).
- **MMPs** (matrix metalloproteinases) break collagen down — UV switches them on strongly.
- Blood vessels, lymphatics, nerves, and appendages: **sebaceous glands** (sebum; androgen-driven), **eccrine sweat glands** (thermoregulation), **apocrine glands** (axilla/groin), **hair follicles**.
- **Dermal–epidermal junction (DEJ)**: wavy rete ridges anchor epidermis; it flattens with age → fragility, slower nutrient exchange.

### Hypodermis
Fat lobules, larger vessels; insulation, energy, cushioning, facial volume. Fat pads shrink and shift with age.

### Functions of skin
Barrier (water, microbes, chemicals, UV), immune defence, thermoregulation, sensation, vitamin D synthesis (UVB), excretion (sweat), and social/communication role.

### Pigmentation
Tyrosinase converts tyrosine → melanin inside melanosomes; melanosomes transfer to keratinocytes and cap the nucleus. **Eumelanin** (brown/black, photoprotective) vs **pheomelanin** (red/yellow, less protective). Triggers: UV, visible (blue) light especially in melasma, inflammation, hormones, some drugs.

### Wound healing (basis of many treatments)
1. Haemostasis → 2. Inflammation (days 1–5) → 3. Proliferation (fibroblasts, new collagen III, new vessels; ~day 4–21) → 4. Remodelling (collagen III replaced by I; months to a year).
Controlled-injury treatments (peels, microneedling, lasers) borrow this process. Too much injury or poor aftercare → scarring or post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH).

## 2. Skin assessment

### Type vs condition
- **Skin type** (largely genetic, stable): normal, dry (low oil), oily, combination.
- **Skin condition** (changeable): dehydrated (low water, can occur in oily skin), sensitised, congested, pigmented, photo-aged, acne-prone, barrier-impaired.

### Fitzpatrick phototype (sun reaction + PIH/treatment risk)
I always burns, never tans · II usually burns · III sometimes burns, tans gradually · IV rarely burns, tans easily · V very rarely burns · VI never burns.
Higher types → higher risk of PIH and burns from IPL/laser and aggressive peels; choose settings and actives accordingly.

### Analysis tools
Imaging systems (e.g. the salon's Observ) use daylight, cross-/parallel-polarised and UV light to show surface texture, subsurface pigment, vascularity, and UV fluorescence (e.g. porphyrins from *C. acnes*, sebum). Use images to set a baseline, explain findings visually and track progress over a course — not to diagnose disease.

### Consultation checklist
Goals and concerns · current routine and products · medical history (skin cancer, eczema, rosacea, autoimmune, diabetes, keloids, herpes simplex/cold sores) · medications (isotretinoin, topical/oral retinoids, antibiotics like doxycycline, photosensitisers, blood thinners, steroids) · pregnancy/breastfeeding · allergies · recent sun/tan/fake tan · recent waxing, peels, injectables, laser · lifestyle (sun, smoking, sleep, stress) · Fitzpatrick type.
Record it, get consent, and repeat key checks each visit.

## 3. Common skin conditions (what a therapist should understand)

**Acne** – Four drivers: (1) excess sebum (androgens), (2) follicular hyperkeratinisation (blocked pores → comedones), (3) *Cutibacterium acnes* overgrowth, (4) inflammation. Grades: comedonal → papulopustular → nodular/cystic. *Salon role:* support the barrier and acid mantle, reduce congestion, LED, home-care; *refer* for nodules/cysts, scarring, sudden adult onset, signs of hormonal disorder, or no improvement after ~3 months. Early effective treatment prevents scarring. Doctor-led options include topical retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, antibiotics (combined, never alone), hormonal therapy, isotretinoin.

**Rosacea** – Chronic inflammatory condition: flushing, persistent redness, visible vessels, papules; triggers include heat, sun, alcohol, spicy food, stress. Barrier-first, gentle care; avoid heat, strong acids, abrasive treatments. IPL can reduce redness/vessels. Medical diagnosis and treatment advised.

**Hyperpigmentation**
- *Solar lentigines* (sun spots) – UV damage; respond well to IPL/peels + SPF.
- *Melasma* – hormonal + UV + visible light, often on cheeks/forehead/upper lip; recurs easily; heat and aggressive IPL can worsen it. Tinted (iron oxide) SPF, tyrosinase inhibitors, tranexamic acid; medical co-management.
- *PIH* – after inflammation (acne, burns, over-treatment); more common in Fitzpatrick IV–VI. Calm inflammation, protect, brighten gently.

**Ageing** – *Intrinsic* (genetic, hormonal: slower turnover, less collagen, thinner skin) and *extrinsic* (UV is the biggest factor, plus smoking, pollution, glycation from sugar). Photoageing: wrinkles, laxity, uneven pigment, broken capillaries, rough texture.

**Dehydration / sensitised skin** – Impaired barrier, tightness, stinging. Repair with gentle cleansing, humectants + ceramides + occlusives; strip back actives.

**Eczema (atopic dermatitis)** – Genetic barrier weakness (often filaggrin), itch, inflammation. Emollients, avoid fragrance and harsh cleansers, medical care for flares.

**Contact dermatitis** – Irritant (over-exfoliation, soaps) or allergic (fragrance, preservatives like methylisothiazolinone, hair dye PPD). Patch test new products.

**Perioral dermatitis** – Small bumps around mouth/nose, often after steroid creams or heavy products; simplify routine, refer.

**Others:** milia (keratin cysts), keratosis pilaris (follicular keratin plugs; responds to AHAs/urea), seborrhoeic dermatitis (*Malassezia*, flaking around nose/brows), sebaceous hyperplasia.

## 4. Ingredients — what they do and how

Format: **Mechanism · Evidence · Use · Cautions**

### Sun protection
- **UV filters** – Chemical/organic filters absorb UV and release it as heat; mineral filters (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) mostly absorb plus some scatter. **SPF** measures UVB (burning) protection; **broad spectrum** = UVA (ageing, pigment) protection too. · *Strong.* · Australia: sunscreens are regulated by the TGA; SPF 50+ is the highest label; use daily when UV index is 3+; about 1 teaspoon for face, neck and ears; reapply every 2 hours and after swimming/sweating. Iron-oxide tints add visible-light protection (useful in melasma). · Most people under-apply, getting far less than the labelled SPF.

### Vitamin A (retinoids)
- **Mechanism:** converted to retinoic acid, which binds nuclear receptors (RAR) and changes gene expression: normalises cell turnover, unblocks follicles, increases collagen, reduces MMPs, evens pigment.
- **Forms (weakest → strongest):** retinyl esters → retinol → retinaldehyde → retinoic acid (tretinoin, prescription). Adapalene is a synthetic retinoid for acne.
- **Evidence:** *Strong* for tretinoin (photoageing, acne); *moderate* for retinol/retinaldehyde.
- **Use:** start low, 2–3 nights/week, build up; night use; always with SPF.
- **Cautions:** dryness, peeling, stinging ("retinisation") for the first weeks. **Avoid in pregnancy** (oral isotretinoin is a known cause of birth defects; topical retinoids are advised against as a precaution). Stop several days before waxing, peels or other resurfacing.
- **Bakuchiol:** plant compound with retinol-like gene effects. One randomised double-blind trial (Br J Dermatol 2019) found 0.5% bakuchiol gave similar improvement in wrinkles and pigment to 0.5% retinol over 12 weeks, with less scaling and stinging. *Limited–moderate* (small study). A gentler option, not a proven equivalent; pregnancy safety data are limited.
- **Hydroxypinacolone retinoate (HPR):** an ester of retinoic acid that binds retinoid receptors without needing conversion; marketed as less irritating. *Limited* (mostly manufacturer and small studies).
- **Encapsulated retinol:** retinol held in a carrier that releases slowly, aiming for the same benefit with less irritation. Still a retinoid — same pregnancy and sun cautions.

### Vitamin C
- **Mechanism:** antioxidant (neutralises UV-generated free radicals), essential cofactor for collagen-making enzymes, inhibits tyrosinase (brightening).
- **Forms:** L-ascorbic acid (best evidence; needs low pH, ~10–20%, unstable — oxidises yellow/orange) and derivatives (more stable, gentler, less evidence).
- **Evidence:** *Moderate.* Works best under SPF in the morning; combinations with vitamin E and ferulic acid improve stability.

### Niacinamide (vitamin B3)
- **Mechanism:** boosts ceramide synthesis (barrier), reduces melanosome transfer (pigment), reduces sebum and redness.
- **Evidence:** *Moderate.* 2–5% is effective and well tolerated. The old warning not to mix it with vitamin C is not a real concern in modern formulas.

### Exfoliating acids
- **AHAs** (water-soluble) loosen bonds between corneocytes, speeding shedding; at higher strengths stimulate dermal collagen. *Glycolic* (smallest molecule, deepest, most irritating), *lactic* (also a humectant, gentler), *mandelic* (large molecule, gentle, good for sensitive and darker skin). Strength depends on both % and pH. Increase sun sensitivity — SPF essential.
- **BHA – salicylic acid** (oil-soluble): penetrates the follicle to clear congestion; mildly anti-inflammatory. Great for oily/acne skin. Avoid high-strength peels in pregnancy as a precaution.
- **PHAs** (gluconolactone, lactobionic acid): large molecules, gentle, humectant and antioxidant.
- **Evidence:** *Strong–moderate.*

### Acne actives
- **Benzoyl peroxide** – releases oxygen that kills *C. acnes*; bacteria do not develop resistance. Bleaches fabric; can dry. 2.5% is as effective as higher strengths with less irritation. *Strong.*
- **Azelaic acid** – antibacterial, normalises keratinisation, anti-inflammatory, inhibits tyrosinase. Useful for acne, rosacea and PIH; generally considered suitable in pregnancy. *Strong–moderate.*
- **Sulfur, tea tree oil** – mild antibacterial/keratolytic; *limited–moderate*; tea tree can sensitise.

### Hydration and barrier
- **Humectants** draw water into the stratum corneum: glycerin (*strong*, inexpensive and excellent), hyaluronic acid/sodium hyaluronate (molecular size changes how deep and how plumping), urea (also gently exfoliating), panthenol.
- **Emollients** smooth and fill gaps between cells: squalane, plant oils, fatty alcohols.
- **Occlusives** form a film that slows water loss: petrolatum (reduces TEWL by up to ~98%), dimethicone, waxes, shea.
- **Barrier lipids:** ceramides, cholesterol and fatty acids work best in combination, close to the skin's natural ratio.

### Brightening / pigment
Tyrosinase inhibitors: arbutin, kojic acid, liquorice extract, vitamin C, azelaic acid, **hexylresorcinol** (potent tyrosinase inhibitor; a 12-week randomised split-face trial by Unilever found hexylresorcinol + niacinamide beat niacinamide alone for pigment spots — *moderate*, manufacturer-run). **Nonapeptide-1** blocks the MC1R receptor so melanocytes receive less 'make pigment' signal — *limited*. **Tranexamic acid** (topical or oral via doctor) reduces signals that drive melasma. **Hydroquinone** is strong but regulated and restricted in Australia; prescription-led only (check current TGA scheduling). Cysteamine has emerging evidence. All need strict SPF or results reverse.

### Peptides
Short amino-acid chains. *Signal peptides* (e.g. palmitoyl pentapeptide-4) tell fibroblasts to make collagen; *carrier peptides* (copper tripeptide-1) aid healing; *neurotransmitter-inhibiting peptides* (acetyl hexapeptide-8) claim to soften expression lines. *Limited–moderate* evidence; often manufacturer-funded.

### Antioxidants and soothing agents
Vitamin E, ferulic acid, green tea (EGCG), resveratrol (antioxidant support, *moderate–limited*). Soothers: panthenol, allantoin, bisabolol, centella asiatica, colloidal oatmeal, zinc.

### Other actives common in salon brands
- **Adenosine** – signalling molecule; around 0.04–0.1% has trial data for smoothing fine lines. *Moderate.*
- **Osmolytes** (betaine, erythritol, trehalose) – help cells hold water under dry conditions. *Limited–moderate.*
- **Sodium PCA, urea, saccharide isomerate, xylitol complexes** – humectants that mimic or support natural moisturising factor. *Moderate.*
- **Zinc oxide (in creams, not SPF)** – mildly astringent, soothing, supports healing. *Moderate* as an adjunct.
- **Witch hazel** – tannins give an astringent, cooling feel; alcohol-based versions can dry. *Limited.*
- **Butcher's broom (ruscogenins)** – vasoconstrictive; used for redness and puffiness. *Limited.*
- **Caffeine** – temporary vasoconstriction, may reduce puffiness. *Limited–moderate.*
- **Enzymes (papain, bromelain)** – digest the protein links between dead cells. *Moderate* for gentle exfoliation.
- **Plant oils** – linoleic-rich oils (evening primrose, hemp, safflower, sunflower) support barrier lipids; oleic-rich and coconut-based oils can congest acne-prone skin.
- **Physical "radiance" pigments** (titanium dioxide, mica, diamond powder) – reflect light for an instant glow. Cosmetic effect only and not sun protection unless the product is a registered sunscreen.

### Pre/pro/postbiotics
Aim to support a balanced microbiome. Mechanism is plausible; evidence is *limited*.

### Common irritants and sensitisers
Fragrance and essential oils, some preservatives (e.g. methylisothiazolinone), high alcohol content, physical scrubs with sharp particles, over-layering actives.

### Combining actives
- Retinoid + AHA/BHA on the same night → irritation risk; alternate nights or use AM/PM.
- Benzoyl peroxide can break down older tretinoin formulas (adapalene is stable with it).
- Vitamin C (AM) + retinoid (PM) + SPF daily is a classic evidence-based routine.
- Any active + compromised barrier = repair first.

## 5. Treatments — what each aims to do and how

| Treatment | How it works | Aims to | Key cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Professional facial** | Cleansing, exfoliation, extraction, massage (see Section 12), masks, targeted actives | Decongest, hydrate, improve circulation, assess and educate | Correct extraction technique to avoid PIH/scarring |
| **Enzyme exfoliation** | Proteases (papain, bromelain) digest surface keratin | Gentle smoothing and brightening | Fruit/latex allergy |
| **Superficial chemical peel** | Acids cause controlled loss of stratum corneum/upper epidermis | Texture, congestion, dullness, mild pigment | Retinoids, isotretinoin, sunburn, active cold sores; strict SPF after. Medium/deep peels are medical procedures |
| **Microdermabrasion / hydrodermabrasion** | Mechanical removal of surface cells (crystals/diamond tip, or water + suction) | Smoothness, product penetration, decongestion | Active acne, rosacea, broken skin |
| **Dermaplaning** | Blade removes dead cells and vellus hair | Smooth surface, makeup sits better | Active acne, sensitivity |
| **Microneedling (collagen induction)** | Micro-injuries trigger wound healing → new collagen and elastin | Scars, texture, fine lines, pores | Infection control, active acne/infection, keloid tendency, isotretinoin; needle depth allowed varies by state and practitioner type |
| **LED light therapy** | Light absorbed by cell components: **blue (~415 nm)** excites porphyrins in *C. acnes* → bacteria-killing free radicals; **red (~630–660 nm)** and **near-infrared (~830 nm)** act on mitochondria → reduced inflammation, supports healing and collagen | Acne support, calming, healing, rejuvenation | Generally safe; caution with photosensitising medications and light-triggered conditions. Evidence *moderate*, best as part of a course |
| **IPL (intense pulsed light)** | Broad-spectrum light (~500–1200 nm, filtered) heats targets by *selective photothermolysis*: melanin (pigment, hair) and haemoglobin (redness, vessels) | Sun spots, redness/capillaries, hair reduction, skin rejuvenation | Tanned skin, higher Fitzpatrick types, melasma (can worsen), photosensitising drugs, isotretinoin, pregnancy, tattoos in area. Tasmania requires a licence for cosmetic IPL — keep current. Patch test |
| **SHR / in-motion hair removal** | Lower-energy repeated pulses gradually heat the follicle | Hair reduction with more comfort | Same contraindications as IPL |
| **Laser** (ablative, non-ablative, fractional) | Single wavelength targets water, pigment or blood | Resurfacing, scars, pigment, vessels | Medical/specialist procedure — refer |
| **Radiofrequency / ultrasound tightening** | Deep heat contracts collagen and triggers remodelling | Laxity, firmness | Metal implants, pacemakers; results gradual |
| **Waxing** | Removes hair from root | Hair removal | Retinoids, isotretinoin, recent peels → skin lifting |

**General aftercare after any active treatment:** gentle cleanser, barrier-repair moisturiser, SPF 50+, no retinoids/acids for several days, no heat (saunas, hot workouts) for 24–48 h, avoid picking.

**Isotretinoin:** conservative salon practice is no waxing, peels, IPL, microneedling or other resurfacing during treatment and for ~6 months after, unless the prescribing doctor advises otherwise.

## 6. Caring for skin through life's stages

**Babies and young children** – Thinner barrier, higher surface area to body weight, so absorb more. Gentle fragrance-free cleansing and emollients. Sun: keep babies under 12 months out of direct sun; widespread sunscreen use under 6 months is not recommended in Australia — shade, clothing and hats come first. Eczema is common; refer flares.

**Puberty / teens** – Androgens increase sebum → acne. Simple routine: gentle cleanser, light moisturiser, SPF, one acne active (BHA, benzoyl peroxide or adapalene). Early medical help prevents scarring. Watch for over-washing and harsh scrubs. Parental/guardian consent for treatments on minors.

**20s** – Build habits: daily SPF, antioxidants, start a gentle retinoid in mid–late 20s if desired. Hormonal acne along the jaw may appear; contraception changes can affect skin.

**30s** – Early photoageing; collagen production falling about 1% per year from adulthood. Retinoids, vitamin C, regular exfoliation, pigment control. Melasma risk with pregnancy and oral contraceptives.

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding** – Common: melasma, acne, sensitivity, stretch marks. *Generally avoid:* retinoids, hydroquinone, high-strength salicylic peels, IPL/laser, most aggressive treatments. *Generally considered suitable:* azelaic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C, low-strength lactic/glycolic, hyaluronic acid, mineral sunscreen, gentle facials and LED (with doctor's approval). Always advise checking with their doctor or midwife.

**Perimenopause and menopause** – Falling oestrogen → rapid collagen loss (about 30% in the first 5 years after menopause, then ~2% a year), thinner, drier, slower-healing skin, more laxity, sometimes adult acne or facial hair from relative androgen excess. Focus on barrier lipids, richer moisturisers, retinoids if tolerated, peptides, SPF, gentle collagen-stimulating treatments. Hormone therapy is a doctor conversation.

**Mature skin (60+)** – Thinner epidermis and dermis, flattened DEJ, reduced sebum and sweat, slower turnover and healing, more fragile vessels (easy bruising), higher skin cancer risk. Gentle cleansing, occlusive moisturisers, lower-strength actives, lower treatment intensity, regular skin checks with a doctor.

**Men** – Thicker skin, more sebum, higher collagen density, shaving irritation and ingrown hairs. Same science applies.

**Skin of colour (Fitzpatrick IV–VI)** – Higher risk of PIH and keloids; still needs sun protection (pigment and ageing). Prefer gentler acids (mandelic, lactic, azelaic), avoid over-treatment, careful device settings.

## 7. Building a home-care routine

Core structure: **Cleanse → Treat (actives) → Hydrate/Repair → Protect (AM SPF)**.
- AM: gentle cleanse, antioxidant (vitamin C), moisturiser, SPF 50+.
- PM: cleanse (double cleanse if wearing SPF/makeup), treatment active (retinoid or acid on alternate nights), moisturiser.
- Introduce one new active at a time, about 2 weeks apart, to identify reactions.
- Match each professional treatment to a home-care range so results continue between visits; review at each appointment and with analysis images. Section 11 maps Ella Baché products to concerns.

## 8. Red flags — refer to a GP or dermatologist

- A mole or spot that is new, changing, irregular, bleeding, or looks different from others (ABCDE: Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter >6 mm, Evolving).
- A sore that does not heal in 3–4 weeks; scaly patches that bleed.
- Nodular/cystic or scarring acne; acne with irregular periods or excess hair growth.
- Signs of infection (heat, spreading redness, pus, fever), active cold sores before treatment.
- Sudden widespread rash, swelling of lips/eyes, or blistering after a product.
- Suspected rosacea, eczema, psoriasis or perioral dermatitis needing medical treatment.
- Any concern that the client is struggling emotionally with their skin — be kind and suggest their GP.

## 9. Talking about skin

- Explain the *why* in plain language ("your barrier is like bricks and mortar…").
- Use the client's analysis images to show, not just tell.
- Set realistic timelines: actives 4–12 weeks; courses of treatments; maintenance.
- Be honest about evidence and limits; never belittle a doctor's treatment plan.
- Compliant wording: "helps reduce the look of", "supports", "aims to" — not "cures", "treats", "fixes" for medical conditions.

## 11. Ella Baché face care range — product knowledge

*Added 2026-10-22 from the ingredient lists, descriptions and therapist tips on ellabache.com.au. Brand claims are reported as claims; the science notes and evidence grades are independent. Formulas change — check the pack INCI if anything looks different, and log updates in Section 10.*

### 11.1 How to read this section
- **Ingredient order matters.** INCI lists run from highest to lowest concentration (down to ~1%, after which order is free). An active listed near the end is usually present at a low level.
- **"Flag"** marks a brand claim that the published ingredient list doesn't clearly support. Don't repeat flagged claims to clients.
- **Allergen notes:** several Classics formulas contain **peanut oil**, tree-nut oils, lanolin, wheat germ oil, bee products or carmine. Always check allergies before recommending.
- **Fragrance** is in almost every product, including the Sensibeautics sensitive range. Patch test for fragrance-allergic or very reactive clients.
- **No day cream in this list has SPF.** Every AM routine ends with a separate SPF 50+.
- **Retinol products** are labelled "prescriptive" by the brand: consult first; not in pregnancy/breastfeeding; not for sensitive skin.

### 11.2 The ranges at a glance

### 11.5 Treatment serums and correctors

**Hydra Hyaluronic Cellular Serum (30 mL)** — Everyday hydration serum.
- *Actives:* sodium hyaluronate + sodium acetylated hyaluronate, glycerin, betaine and erythritol (osmolytes), aloe, hibiscus.
- *Use:* AM/PM under moisturiser on face, neck, décolleté; can go around outer eye. Layers with other serums.
- *For:* most skin types, dehydration. No silicones, parabens or phenoxyethanol. *Note:* hibiscus "natural acids" are too low to exfoliate meaningfully.

**Hyaluronic Intensive Treatment Booster (30 mL)** — Concentrated, fragrance-free hydration.
- *Actives:* sodium hyaluronate + crosslinked HA (five molecular weights), hydroxyethyl urea, sodium polyglutamate, spirulina, adenosine.
- *Why:* smaller HA reaches further into the stratum corneum; larger HA holds water on the surface. Claims of reaching "deep layers" overstate what topical HA does.
- *Use:* 1–2 drops AM/PM after cleansing, then serum and moisturiser; brand suggests a 7-day intensive.
- *For:* all, including sensitised and post-treatment dehydrated skin (no fragrance).

**NeoBright Correcting Serum (30 mL)** — Pigment and tone corrector.
- *Actives:* **hexylresorcinol** (high in list — the main tyrosinase inhibitor), Kakadu plum extract, papain (enzyme resurfacing), niacinamide and adenosine (low), hydrolysed HA, bisabolol.
- *Why:* blocks the enzyme that makes melanin while gently loosening pigmented surface cells.
- *Use:* pearl amount AM/PM after cleansing; follow with NeoBright cream. SPF 50+ daily.
- *For:* pigmentation, sun damage, dullness. *Not for:* sensitive skin. *Note:* its "vitamin C" is Kakadu plum extract, not ascorbic acid. Pigment treatment needs 8–12+ weeks and strict SPF.

**NeoBright Radiance C Ampoule Set (13 mL + 3.6 g)** — Fresh-mix vitamin C course.
- *Actives:* powder of **pure ascorbic acid** + liquorice, pomegranate, tomato, white tea; liquid with lactic, salicylic and malic acids, **nonapeptide-1** (pigment-signal blocker), urea, sodium PCA, trehalose, sodium hyaluronate, alcohol.
- *Why two parts:* L-ascorbic acid oxidises quickly once dissolved, so it is mixed fresh to stay potent.
- *Use:* shake powder, tip into serum, shake to dissolve; apply to clean face avoiding eyes; shake each use; finish within 14 days; store below 25 °C out of sun. Pair with NeoBright serum and creams; SPF 50+.
- *For:* dull, uneven, sun-exposed skin. *Caution:* alcohol and acids may sting sensitive skin.

**Detox Aromatique Extra-Purifying Corrector Serum (30 mL)** — Oil-control and blemish serum.
- *Actives:* **salicylic acid** (5th — a real BHA level), oleanolic acid (sebum-regulating claim), ethanol (2nd), a long list of essential oils (orange, grapefruit, lavender, thyme, eucalyptus and others).
- *Why:* salicylic acid is oil-soluble and clears inside the pore (*strong* evidence).
- *Use:* under moisturiser AM/PM as a 1–2 month course.
- *For:* oily, congested, acne-prone. *Not for:* sensitive or barrier-impaired skin. *Caution:* alcohol + essential oils (limonene, linalool, geraniol) can dry and sensitise; SPF.

**SpirulinesLift Intensive Firming Serum (30 mL)** — Early-ageing serum with a retinol alternative.
- *Actives:* bifida ferment lysate, spirulina, maca, chlorella/lupin protein ferment, **bakuchiol**, sodium hyaluronate, adenosine, beta-glucan, algin.
- *Use:* 1–2 pumps face and neck AM/PM before moisturiser.
- *For:* all types, early lines; a choice for clients who can't tolerate retinol. *Note:* lactic acid is at the very end — not an exfoliating level.

**Eternal+ Sculpting Serum (30 mL)** — Firming serum for mature skin.
- *Actives:* acacia gum and cellulose (film-formers giving an instant tightening feel), xylitol humectant complex, shampoo-ginger extract, adenosine, acetyl dipeptide-1 cetyl ester, hydroxyproline, aspartic acid, silanetriol, cranberry.
- *Use:* AM/PM face, neck, décolleté under moisturiser; layer with Hyaluronic Booster.

**Sensibeautics Intensive Recovery Serum (30 mL)** — Calming serum for redness.
- *Actives:* alphaglucan oligosaccharide (prebiotic), Lactobacillus (postbiotic lysate), yacon juice, hydrolysed linseed, Cestrum latifolium, grape leaf, brown and red algae, wheat germ and raspberry oils; light-reflecting mica/titanium dioxide.

### 11.11 Matching ranges to concerns (quick guide)
- **Dehydrated (any type):** Hyaluronic Booster + Hydra serum/creams; Hyaluronic Regeneration Mask; Daily Hydration Mist.
- **Pigmentation/dullness:** NeoBright serum + day/night creams; Radiance C Ampoule course; Enzyme Exfoliant; SPF 50+ every day (non-negotiable). Melasma: avoid heat, be gentle, co-manage with GP.
- **Lines and texture, retinoid-ready:** Retinol Night Serum ± Retinol Oil, buffered with Emulsion Tomate; Retinol Eye Serum over Special Eye Cream.
- **Early ageing / retinol-intolerant / prefer gentler:** SpirulinesLift range (bakuchiol).
- **Mature, dry, menopausal:** Eternal+ range; Reconstructing Very Rich Cream; Intensive Recovery Cream as a barrier balm.
- **Sensitive/redness:** Sensibeautics serum + Daily Resistance or Rescue Cream; Emulsion Tomate; Radiance Foaming Cleanser or Moisture-Rich Milk; avoid alcohol toners, scrubs, acids. IPL for visible vessels.
- **Oily/congested/acne-prone:** Radiance Foaming Cleanser; Intensive Balancing Lotion (if barrier tolerates); Detox Aromatique serum (course); Breakout Treatment Cream on spots; Enzyme Exfoliant or Multi-Acid Peel; light Emulsion Tomate; LED in salon. Avoid heavy Classics creams. Deep or persistent acne → doctor.
- **Post-IPL/SHR/waxing:** Recovery Gel; Hyaluronic Booster; Sensibeautics Rescue Cream; no acids/retinol for several days; SPF 50+.
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** avoid all three Retinol products; check with doctor before NeoBright Ampoule (salicylic), Detox Aromatique and Multi-Acid Peel; Hydra, Sensibeautics, Emulsion Tomate and gentle cleansers are the safest fit.

## 12. Traditional facial massage — hand movements, traditions and evidence

*Added 2026-10-22. History is reported with how well documented it is; benefits carry evidence grades. Most massage research is on the body, not the face; facial studies are few, small and short.*

### 12.1 What massage actually does to skin (the biology)
- **Mechanoreceptors and nerves:** light rhythmic touch stimulates skin receptors and tends to shift the nervous system towards a calmer, parasympathetic state. *Moderate* (mainly body-massage research).
- **Blood flow:** a Japanese study (Hayashi, Complement Ther Med 2018) found 5 minutes of roller massage raised cheek blood flow by up to ~25% for over 10 minutes; 5 weeks of daily massage improved the vessels' dilation response to heat. Warmth, pinkness and "glow" after a facial are mostly this. *Moderate, short-term.*
- **Lymph:** facial lymphatics have no pump; they rely on movement and pressure. Lymph from the face drains to nodes in front of the ear, under the jaw and chin, and down the neck to the collarbone area (the terminus). Very light directional strokes can shift fluid and reduce puffiness for hours, not permanently. *Limited–moderate.*
- **Muscle:** kneading and pressure can ease tension in the masseter (jaw), temples and forehead. Some evidence in jaw-pain (TMD) research. *Limited–moderate.* Massage does not "build" or "tone" facial muscles in the way exercise does.
- **Product application:** massage spreads and works product into the surface; claims that it pushes actives "deep into the skin" are overstated — penetration depends on the molecule, not the hands.
- **What massage does *not* do:** remove "toxins", melt fat, reshape bone, or permanently lift sagging skin. No good evidence for lasting wrinkle reduction.

### 12.2 Western (classical/European) facial massage

**Origin.** Modern European massage grew from 19th-century physical medicine. Pehr Henrik Ling (Sweden) built a system of medical gymnastics in which massage was a small part. Dutch physician Johann Mezger (1838–1909) developed systematic manual treatment in Amsterdam; his students named the classic strokes with French terms. Mezger's method later became widely, and inaccurately, called "Swedish massage". Facial massage in beauty therapy adapts these strokes to the face.

**The core movements**

| Movement | How it's done on the face | What it aims to do | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Effleurage** | Light, even, gliding strokes with flat palms or finger pads; opens and closes every sequence and links movements | Relax, spread product, warm tissue, assess skin | Safest stroke; the only one for very sensitive, rosacea or post-treatment skin |
| **Petrissage** | Kneading, rolling, lifting and squeezing — e.g. small kneading along the jaw, lifting cheeks between fingers and thumb | Stimulate circulation, ease muscle tension, sebum flow | Firmer; avoid over active acne, broken capillaries, thin or fragile skin |
| **Friction** | Small circular or rolling movements with finger pads that move skin over underlying tissue (e.g. chin, nose, forehead circles) | Stimulate, loosen congestion, warmth | Keep light on thin skin; don't drag |
| **Tapotement (percussion)** | On the face only light "piano" finger tapping, usually around the jaw, cheeks and under the eyes | Stimulating, toning feel, wakes skin up | Never hacking/cupping on the face; avoid on sensitive, rosacea, capillaries |
| **Vibration** | Fine trembling of fingers held on one point | Soothing or stimulating depending on speed; often near nerve points | Gentle; useful over tense areas |
| **Pressure points / static holds** | Slow, sustained finger pressure (e.g. temples, under cheekbones, brow) | Relaxation, a "grounding" finish | Western technique borrowed from Eastern acupressure |

**Principles taught in beauty therapy**
- Work **upwards and outwards** on the face, supporting tissue rather than dragging it down (exception: lymph drainage strokes go towards the nodes).
- Keep **contact and rhythm** — a hand stays on the skin; even tempo.
- **Slow = relaxing; fast = stimulating.** Choose by skin and goal.
- **Pressure lighter on the face than the body**, lightest around the eyes (ring finger, outer to inner corner under the eye along the orbital bone).
- Use enough slip (oil or cream) to avoid pulling, except for dry techniques like Jacquet.
- Typical order: décolleté and shoulders → neck (upward) → jawline and chin → cheeks → nose → around eyes → forehead → temples → finishing effleurage and pressure holds.

**Jacquet (pinching) massage**
- *How:* dry skin, quick light pinches between thumb and index finger, rolling the skin up and releasing, usually over oily T-zones and cheeks.
- *Aims:* stimulate circulation and help empty sebaceous follicles in oily, congested skin.
- *Origin:* named after a French dermatologist (usually linked to Lucien Jacquet, 1860–1914), but the technique's history is poorly documented and sources conflict.
- *Cautions:* not over pustules or inflamed acne (spreads infection, raises PIH risk); not on sensitive, rosacea or capillary-prone skin. *Evidence: theoretical/limited.*

**Manual lymphatic drainage (MLD)**
- *Origin:* developed by Danish couple Emil and Estrid Vodder, who began studying lymph in 1932 and presented the method in Paris in 1936.
- *How:* very light pressure (just enough to stretch the skin), slow, rhythmic, stationary circles and pumping strokes. Always **clear the exit first** — start at the collarbone/neck terminus, then neck, jaw, face — then move fluid towards the nodes.
- *Evidence:* best evidence is medical, for lymphoedema as part of combined therapy (*moderate*). For cosmetic facial puffiness, *limited* — effects are short-lived.
- *Contraindications:* acute infection or inflammation, untreated heart failure, blood clots, active cancer (needs medical clearance), uncontrolled thyroid conditions, acute kidney problems.

### 12.3 Eastern facial traditions

**Chinese**
- **Tui na and acupressure** — part of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). Pressure is applied on points along "meridians", believed to channel *qi* (vital energy). Meridians are a traditional framework; they have not been found as physical structures. Acupressure-style holds can be deeply relaxing; evidence for specific facial or skin outcomes is *limited*.
- **Gua sha** — traditionally a firm scraping of the back and body with a smooth tool to deliberately raise petechiae ("sha"). The modern facial version uses a stone tool with oil and **light** pressure, gliding outwards and towards the lymph nodes.
  - *Evidence:* a 2007 body study (11 people, no control) found surface blood flow rose about fourfold for ~25 minutes. A 2025 Korean randomised trial (34 women, 8 weeks, gua sha vs roller, no untreated control) found small contour and muscle-tone changes with gua sha and elasticity gains with a roller. A 2023 systematic review found the evidence too weak to support anti-ageing claims. *Limited.*
  - *Risks:* bruising and broken capillaries if pressed hard (face capillaries are superficial), PIH, rosacea flares, displacing fresh filler.
- **Jade/stone rolling** — mostly a modern beauty trend; claims of ancient imperial use are poorly documented. A cooled roller can briefly reduce puffiness; roller massage increases blood flow (see Hayashi). Keep tools clean (alcohol wipe between uses).

**Japanese**
- **Anma** — traditional Japanese massage derived from Chinese tui na.
- **Kobido** ("ancient way of beauty") — its own lineage story places its origin in 1472 from a contest between two Anma masters, later reserved for the imperial court. This story isn't independently documented. Today it is a fast, rhythmic, choreographed facial with 40+ techniques: alternating slow sculpting strokes with quick percussive tapping, lifting sequences along jaw, cheeks and temples, acupressure, and lymphatic strokes towards the neck. Sessions run 45–60 minutes. *Evidence: no clinical trials; mechanisms are those of massage generally.*
- **Shiatsu** — finger-pressure therapy formalised in 20th-century Japan; facial shiatsu uses sustained thumb/finger pressure on points.
- **Modern lymphatic "face sculpting" methods** (e.g. the popular Japanese self-massage known as Asahi/Zogan) — firmer lymphatic strokes for de-puffing. Watch pressure on thin or mature skin.

**Indian (Ayurvedic)**
- **Mukha abhyanga** — facial massage with warm herbal oils chosen by *dosha* (constitution type), part of daily self-care in Ayurveda.
- **Marma therapy** — gentle pressure on *marma* points (vital junctions in Ayurvedic tradition, many on the face and head).
- *Evidence:* relaxation benefits plausible; specific health claims about doshas and marma *traditional/theoretical*. Heavy oils may congest acne-prone skin.

**Other**
- **Vietnamese Dien Chan** (facial reflexology, developed in Vietnam in the 1980s) — stimulates facial points believed to reflect body organs. *No scientific evidence* for the organ-mapping claims.
- **Buccal / intraoral sculpting massage** — modern technique working cheek muscles from inside the mouth (gloved). Relaxes jaw and cheek muscles; *limited* evidence. Needs specific training and strict hygiene; check local scope of practice.

### 12.4 Different points of view
- **Traditional/energetic view (TCM, Ayurveda, Kobido):** massage balances energy (*qi*, *prana*), clears stagnation and supports organ health and longevity. Valuable as a cultural and holistic framework; not supported by anatomical or clinical evidence. Present it as "in traditional Chinese medicine it's believed…", not as fact.
- **Biomedical view:** massage acts through nerves, circulation, fluid movement and muscle relaxation. Benefits are real but mostly short-term and modest; anti-ageing claims are overstated.
- **Sceptical dermatology view:** rubbing and pulling ageing skin may do more harm than good; vigorous tools cause capillary damage and pigmentation; claims of "sculpting" and "detox" are marketing.
- **Wellbeing view:** the relaxation and human touch of a facial are benefits in themselves — stress affects skin (barrier repair slows, flares of acne, eczema, rosacea). This is a legitimate reason to massage that doesn't need inflated claims. See Section 13.
- **Facial exercise vs massage:** a small 2018 Northwestern study (JAMA Dermatology; 27 enrolled, 16 completed) found 20 weeks of daily-then-alternate-day facial exercises improved cheek fullness and estimated age by ~3 years. This was *active exercise*, not massage. *Limited.*

### 12.5 Benefits and drawbacks at a glance

| Benefit | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Relaxation, stress relief, client experience | *Moderate* (mostly body studies) |
| Temporary increase in skin blood flow, glow | *Moderate*, short-term |
| Temporary reduction in puffiness (lymph) | *Limited–moderate*, hours |
| Easing jaw/forehead tension | *Limited–moderate* |
| Even product application, skin assessment by touch | Practical |
| Elasticity / contour change | *Limited* (single small trials) |
| Lasting wrinkle reduction, collagen building, "lifting", detox | *Not supported* |

| Drawback / risk | Who's most at risk |
|---|---|
| Pulling and stretching thin skin | Mature skin, eye area, heavy-handed technique |
| Redness, flushing, flare | Rosacea, sensitised, post-peel or retinol skin |
| Spreading infection, scarring, PIH | Active pustular acne, impetigo, cold sores, warts |
| Bruising, broken capillaries | Gua sha, pinching, firm pressure; blood thinners; fragile capillaries |
| Displacing fresh filler or anti-wrinkle injection | Recent injectables — follow the injector's advice; commonly no firm massage over treated areas for ~2 weeks after filler, and no rubbing for at least 24 h after anti-wrinkle injections |
| Congestion from heavy oils | Acne-prone, oily skin |
| Misleading claims | Any marketing that promises lifting, sculpting or detox (TGA/ACL) |

### 12.6 Contraindications checklist (no facial massage, or modify)
Contagious or active skin infection (cold sores, impetigo, fungal, warts) · inflamed pustular acne (avoid those areas) · open wounds, recent sunburn, recent peel/microneedling/IPL on the same day · active rosacea flare or marked broken capillaries (effleurage only, light, cool) · recent facial surgery or injectables · undiagnosed lumps or swollen glands (refer) · fever or feeling unwell · severe eczema/dermatitis flare · uncontrolled blood pressure or recent heart events (gentle only, medical advice) · lymphatic drainage-specific contraindications (12.2) · allergy to the massage medium.

### 12.7 Matching movements to skin
- **Sensitive / rosacea / post-IPL:** slow effleurage and static holds only, light pressure, cool hands, no tapotement, no friction, no tools; fragrance-free medium (e.g. Hyaluronic Booster or a calming cream).
- **Dehydrated:** effleurage + gentle MLD; humectant serum under a cream.
- **Oily / congested (no active pustules):** petrissage and light Jacquet over oily zones; friction circles on chin and nose; light medium.
- **Acne with active lesions:** work around lesions, effleurage and MLD only; LED after; never massage over pustules.
- **Mature / thin skin:** supportive upward strokes, lifting petrissage with plenty of slip, gentle eye work with ring finger, no dragging, light tools only.
- **Puffy:** MLD sequence (terminus first), cool roller or chilled stones; results are temporary — say so.
- **Tense jaw / clencher:** slow kneading and pressure on masseter and temples (refer suspected TMJ disorder or teeth grinding to dentist/GP).

### 12.8 Talking about massage with clients
- Sell the real benefits honestly: relaxation, glow, de-puffing, comfort, and a chance to assess the skin.
- Avoid "lifts", "sculpts", "detoxes", "builds collagen", "reshapes your face". Use "helps you look refreshed", "temporarily reduces puffiness", "relaxes tense facial muscles".
- Describe traditions respectfully and accurately: "This technique comes from Japanese Kobido, which traditionally aims to balance energy; from a skin-science point of view it boosts circulation and relaxes the face."

## 13. Benefits of human touch to the receiver

*Added 2026-10-22. Short summary; evidence graded.*

- **Overall:** a 2024 meta-analysis (Packheiser et al., *Nature Human Behaviour*; ~130 studies, ~10,000 people) found wanted touch improves wellbeing. Strongest effects: less pain, anxiety and low mood. Smaller effects: blood pressure and heart rate. *Moderate–strong.*
- **Head and face touch did best** of all body areas — directly relevant to facials.
- **Human beats object** (robots, weighted blankets) for mental-health benefits.
- **A professional's touch works as well as a loved one's** for adults.
- **More often beats longer:** no extra benefit beyond ~20 minutes per session; regular sessions matter more.
- **Why it works:** skin has "caress" nerves (C-tactile afferents) in hairy skin, including the face. They fire best with slow strokes of about 3 cm/second (range 1–10) at skin temperature (~32 °C) and feed brain areas linked to emotion.
- **Over-claimed:** "massage lowers cortisol by 31%" came from uncontrolled studies; a rigorous 2011 review found small, mostly non-significant changes. Oxytocin rises are reported in small studies only. *Limited* — don't promise hormone effects.
- **In the treatment room:** warm hands; slow, even, continuous strokes; include face, scalp and neck; ask consent and check comfort; respect clients who want less touch (trauma, culture, sensory sensitivity).
- **Compliant wording:** "relaxing, can help ease tension and lift your mood" — not "lowers stress hormones" or "detoxes".

## 14. Australia: claims, consent and law

*Added 2026-11-04. General information gathered from regulators' own pages, checked 4 Nov 2026. **Not legal advice.** Where something matters (a new device, a new claim, a complaint), the owner checks with head office or a lawyer. Ella Baché head office's approved claims, once supplied, override this section.*

### 14.1 The line between cosmetic and therapeutic
- **The test is appearance vs physiology.** The TGA says cosmetic claims concern how skin looks; therapeutic claims concern "influencing, inhibiting or modifying a physiological process". A product (or the way we describe it) becomes a therapeutic good when it claims to prevent, diagnose or treat a disease or its symptoms, or to affect the structure or function of the body. Source: TGA, *Cosmetics or therapeutic goods: guidance for advertisers and suppliers* v1.0 (Jun 2024).
- **TGA's own paired examples** (use these as the model):

| Cosmetic (OK) | Therapeutic (not OK for a cosmetic) |
|---|---|
| "Helps skin look firm" | "Improves skin's internal structure for firmer skin" |
| "Helps your skin look even and smooth" | "Reduces skin pigmentation and uneven skin tone" |
| "Contains collagen to smooth the appearance of fine lines" | "Stimulates collagen production to repair skin at the cellular level" |

- **Context counts.** A cosmetic claim such as "soothes dry skin" becomes therapeutic if it sits next to images of psoriasis or on a page about a skin disease (TGA guidance). So a rosacea or eczema blog post must not sit beside a "shop the cream" link that implies the cream treats it.
- **Named conditions are therapeutic territory:** treating or relieving pimples and acne, eczema, psoriasis, rosacea; promoting hair growth or reducing hair loss (TGA guidance).
- **Acne:** some anti-acne products are allowed as cosmetics only where they work by cleansing (or similar actions such as moisturising, exfoliating, drying) under the Therapeutic Goods (Excluded Goods) Determination 2018; the full conditions are in the Determination [UNVERIFIED wording: check the Determination or head office before writing any acne product claim]. Our wording: "breakout-prone", "congested", "helps keep skin clean and balanced". Never "treats", "clears" or "cures" acne. The salon's acne approach (protect the acid mantle, LED, work alongside the doctor) is described as care and support, not treatment of a disease.
- **Pigment:** "reduces pigmentation" is a TGA example of a therapeutic claim. Say "helps skin look more even" or "targets the appearance of sun spots". Hydroquinone-type melanin inhibitors are medicines.
- **Sunscreen:** primary sunscreens (mainly for UV protection) are therapeutic goods regulated by the TGA (AICIS). Moisturisers with SPF that make a therapeutic claim such as "helps protect skin from the damaging effects of UV radiation" are regulated as medicines. Quote SPF and protection claims **exactly as the product label says**, nothing more.

### 14.2 Devices and treatments: Tasmanian rules
- **IPL/SHR needs a radiation licence in Tasmania.** The Tasmanian Department of Health lists "IPL used for cosmetic purposes" (and Class 3B/4 lasers) as radiation sources; using or possessing one without a licence under the Radiation Protection Act 2005 is an offence. Applications are assessed on the operator's qualifications, experience and skills; the guideline is being updated, so check current requirements with the Radiation Protection Unit. Tasmania publishes a list of accredited IPL and cosmetic laser operators. **Only licensed therapists operate the SHR/IPL; copy must not suggest otherwise.** Confirm who at the salon holds a current licence [owner to confirm].
- **Skin penetration needs a council licence.** Under the Public Health Act 1997 (Tas), a "public health risk activity" (tattooing, "other skin penetration and body modification procedures") needs a licence from the local council (City of Hobart), renewed every 12 months. Microneedling is likely to count as skin penetration [UNVERIFIED: confirm with City of Hobart]. Waxing that may draw blood and lash or brow work may also be covered by council rules [owner to confirm].
- **Describe devices accurately.** The machine is SHR/IPL, not a laser. Don't call LED, microdermabrasion or microneedling "medical" or "clinical-grade" unless head office approves the wording.

### 14.3 Australian Consumer Law (ACCC)
- **No misleading claims or results promises.** "Guaranteed", "permanent", "instant results", "pain-free" only if literally true for everyone. Results claims need evidence; say "results vary".
- **Reviews and testimonials** (ACCC online reviews page, updated 15 Jul 2026): reviews must be genuine and reflect the reviewer's own experience; undisclosed reviews by staff, family or paid people mislead; editing a review can mislead; incentives must be given whether the review is positive or negative and must be disclosed; never suppress, edit or remove genuine negative reviews. (Google's own policy goes further: no incentives at all, no gating; see ella-bache-local-seo.)
- **Before-and-after photos** are claims: only real, unedited, representative results, with written client consent and head-office approval.
- **Gift vouchers** (ACCC): vouchers sold since 1 Nov 2019 must be redeemable for **at least 3 years**, and the expiry date must be shown prominently (with the issue date if it's a period; "No expiry date" if none). Exceptions include promotional or genuinely discounted vouchers. Check the salon's vouchers meet this [owner to confirm the voucher terms in ella-bache-business].
- **Faulty products:** consumer guarantees under the ACL apply whatever the shop policy says; never write a policy that implies otherwise.

### 14.4 Regulated health services
- Advertising rules for registered health practitioners (Ahpra, e.g. bans on testimonials) apply to practitioners such as doctors and nurses, not to beauty therapists. If a registered nurse or doctor ever performs or is named in a treatment, those rules apply to that advertising [check before writing].
- Cosmetic injectables are out of scope for the salon: never mention them.

### 14.5 Privacy (client records, consultation forms, photos)
- The Privacy Act's small-business exemption **does not apply to a health service provider that holds health information** (OAIC). A health service includes assessing or managing a person's physical health and recording health information for that purpose; the OAIC names massage and other complementary therapists as health service providers. A salon that records medications, pregnancy, skin conditions and contraindications on consultation forms may be covered [UNVERIFIED: owner to confirm with head office or an adviser]. Until confirmed, treat consultation records as if the Privacy Act applies: collect only what's needed, keep them secure, and never discuss a client's skin, treatment or attendance in public (including in review replies).
- Photos of clients (social, before-and-after, Observ images) need written consent for each use.

### 14.6 Words to use and avoid
| Avoid | Use instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| cures, treats (a condition), heals, fixes | helps, supports, soothes, cares for | Treating a disease is a therapeutic claim (TGA) |
| clears acne, acne treatment | for breakout-prone or congested skin; helps keep skin clean and balanced | Acne is a named therapeutic condition (TGA) |
| reduces pigmentation, removes sun damage | helps skin look more even; targets the appearance of sun spots | TGA's own therapeutic example |
| repairs, regenerates, stimulates collagen, builds collagen | smooths the look of fine lines; skin looks firmer | Physiological change = therapeutic (TGA) |
| detox, lifts, sculpts | refreshes, relaxes, temporarily reduces puffiness | Unsupported; misleading (ACL) |
| guaranteed, permanent, instant results | results vary; a course of treatments is usually recommended | Misleading results promise (ACL) |
| laser (for our SHR/IPL) | SHR/IPL | Accuracy; it's not a laser |
| medical-grade, clinical, prescription | professional, in-salon | Implies therapeutic status; head office to approve |
| protects against skin cancer | as the sunscreen label says, exactly | SPF claims must match the label (TGA) |
